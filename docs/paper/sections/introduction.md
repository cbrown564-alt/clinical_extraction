# Introduction draft

Epilepsy clinic letters contain diagnoses, seizure types and frequencies, medicines, investigations, and treatment changes that are often absent from consistently structured fields. Converting this information into structured data can support cohort construction, retrospective review, and longitudinal outcome research (Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2019; Xie et al., 2023). Manual review is valuable but difficult to scale. The present study examines methods for structuring narrative letters; it does not propose a deployed clinical decision-support system or replace clinical judgement.

A useful epilepsy research record needs complementary information: a broad phenotype covering diagnoses, seizure types, medicines, investigations, and related facts, together with an account of seizure burden or current control. Seizure frequency is a core clinical outcome measure for assessing control and treatment response (Decker et al., 2022), but letters may contain several true statements for different seizure types, periods, or levels of certainty. Turning such narrative into a defined record therefore involves three decisions: finding candidate statements, translating them into the required structured form, and deciding what enters the final record. The two tasks used here reflect these complementary needs: ExECT represents a multi-fact clinical inventory, whereas Gan represents one current seizure-frequency state (Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2024; Gan et al., 2026).

Recent epilepsy NLP often contrasts manually written rules with learned methods, although hybrid systems already combine them (Yew et al., 2023). Rules state vocabulary, time windows, and inclusion choices explicitly, but depend on local wording. Learned models interpret varied and temporally distributed narrative more flexibly, but are harder to constrain and inspect. The central design question is how learned interpretation and written task rules should work together: which component should find evidence, translate it into the required form, and make each clinically consequential final choice.

This study evaluates the three-decision framework on two public epilepsy-letter datasets with different sources and output forms (Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2024; Gan et al., 2026). It compares alternative divisions of work among written rules, learned models, and their combinations, while retaining quoted evidence and records of each transformation to the submitted answer. Public data permit other researchers to replicate or re-evaluate the comparison. The design addresses the limitations of private, single-site evaluation but does not isolate institutional transfer because data source, task definition, and annotation policy differ together.
