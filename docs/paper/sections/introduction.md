# Introduction draft

Scope: [paper-story simplification](../decisions/paper-story-simplification.md)

Epilepsy affects about 1% of the UK population and is characterised by recurrent, unprovoked seizures. Seizure frequency is a key indicator of disease burden, is used in clinical care to guide treatment, and is a primary outcome in clinical trials. Despite that role, the only place it is routinely recorded is the clinic letter: an unstructured narrative of a recent visit that combines past diagnoses, current symptoms and future treatment. That information could support retrospective studies and cohort identification, but extracting it into structured form requires costly, error-prone manual chart review.

Rules-based methods have extracted medications and diagnoses from clinic letters but struggled with the imprecise language and diverse forms of seizure-frequency descriptions (Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2024). *2 to 3 seizures a week*, *frequent bouts of seizures* and *4 since her last visit* are all valid descriptions of the same fact, and rules that cover them completely and consistently are hard to write. Rules remain valued because they are interpretable and can carry local expertise, but a different tool is needed for the semantic part of the task. Large language models (LLMs) perform far better on such tasks from instructions and a few examples alone, including seizure-frequency extraction (Gan et al., 2026). Their outputs, unlike those of rules, are not constrained to the text they were given: LLMs can invent evidence or answer a different question (Holgate et al., 2025). Fine-tuning reduces this error but needs substantial training data and computation. Prior work has used rules only as a light repair layer after an LLM; none has given rules a decision role as an alternative to fine-tuning.

Extracting one label is not one step. A letter can describe several seizure events with varying precision and on different occasions, so the system must first **extract** every candidate fact with its evidence, then **decide** which candidate, or which combination, answers the question. Which to keep is a clinical policy that varies between users. Keeping the two stages apart captures more evidence than a single answer would, lets one extraction serve several policies, and makes the final answer explainable: a reader sees the candidates, the policy and the label it produced.

The primary objective is to evaluate whether a prompted LLM and rules can extract seizure-frequency patterns from epilepsy letters as accurately as the fine-tuned LLM previously reported on the same corpus. The secondary objective is to test whether applying a decision to the extraction record improves on the provisional answer, whether it matters if rules or a second LLM call apply the policy, and which ingredients of the extraction prompt the result depends on.

The pipeline was scored after each stage on 1,200 synthetic epilepsy clinic letters from Gan et al. (2026), each with one expert-annotated label for the patient's current seizure-frequency pattern. Results are compared with the benchmark Gan et al. report for their fine-tuned LLM. The two papers use different held-out samples from the same synthetic corpus; the results section reports the uncertainty this introduces. Everything reported is on synthetic data; no claim is made about real-letter performance, clinical validity, workflow fit, privacy compliance, or readiness to deploy.
