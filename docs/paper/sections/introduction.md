An epilepsy clinic letter is a short narrative, about 400 words, that a specialist writes after seeing a patient. It records the patient's history, how they are doing now and what treatment is planned. For research, the most useful fact in that letter is usually how often the patient is having seizures: seizure frequency is the main measure of how well epilepsy is controlled and the usual outcome in clinical trials (Fisher et al., 2014). Epilepsy affects about 1% of the UK population (Wigglesworth et al., 2023), so these letters exist in large numbers, but the frequency is rarely recorded anywhere else in a structured form. A study that needs it must have experts read every letter by hand, which is slow and inconsistent.

Large language models (LLMs) have made this task feasible. Given instructions and a few examples, they can read a letter and report a frequency whether it is written as *2 to 3 seizures a week*, *frequent bouts* or *4 since her last visit*. Two problems remain. First, an LLM's answer is not always based on the letter in front of it: in one study a model asked for the seizure frequency instead gave advice on the patient's medication (Holgate et al., 2025). Second, the most accurate published result on this task came from a model fine-tuned on more than a thousand labelled letters (Gan et al., 2026), and labelled clinical text is expensive to produce. A method that reached the same accuracy from instructions alone, and showed where in the letter each answer came from, would remove both costs.

There is a further difficulty that is easy to miss. A letter often mentions more than one frequency: the rate last year, the rate since a medication change, or separate rates for different seizure types. Which of these is the patient's current frequency is a clinical judgement, and different studies may want different answers. Clinical information extraction has long been described as two steps, finding a mention in the text and converting it to a standard form; this task has a third, choosing between the mentions found. When a single prompt is asked for one final label, all three steps happen inside the model and the choice cannot be seen, checked or changed. This paper separates them. An LLM first extracts every frequency mentioned in the letter, together with the text it came from; a fixed, written policy then decides which one is the answer. Because the policy works on the extracted record rather than on the letter, it can be applied by rules or by a second LLM call.

The aim of this paper is to find out whether that design can match the fine-tuned benchmark. It asks four questions: whether the pipeline reaches the F1 that Gan et al. reported; whether the decision step improves on the answer the extraction call gives on its own; whether it matters if rules or a second LLM call apply the policy; and which parts of the extraction prompt the result depends on. The contribution is the two-stage design, its comparison with the benchmark, and an account of which component the result depends on. The evaluation uses the 1,200 synthetic letters released by Gan et al. and their stricter scoring setting. On a held-out set of 450 letters, both versions of the pipeline exceeded the reported benchmark (F1 0.86 and 0.85 against 0.81) and both improved on the extraction call's own answer (0.79). The two studies used different held-out samples from the same corpus, so this is a comparison with a published result rather than a paired test, and because the letters are synthetic the results say nothing yet about real clinical text.

The literature review places this split against earlier extractors. The methods describe the letters, the two stages and how they are scored. The results report the benchmark comparison, the lift from decide, the prompt ablations and a check across six models. The discussion and ethical section set out what follows from those results, and what does not.
