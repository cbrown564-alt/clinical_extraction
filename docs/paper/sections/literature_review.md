# Literature review draft

Revised: 2026-09-02 (Gan only; extract then decide). Matches
`paper/draft/FES.tex` Section II. The extended review is in

Scope: [paper-story simplification](../decisions/paper-story-simplification.md)

The best approach to extracting seizure-frequency information from epilepsy clinic letters is an active area of research, with rules-based methods (Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2024; Decker et al., 2022), transformer-based methods (Xie et al., 2022; Holgate et al., 2025; Gan et al., 2026) and combinations of both (Xie et al., 2022b; Fernandes et al., 2024) all published this decade. Rules-based methods struggled with the lexical variety of frequency descriptions. Both rules and BERT-based methods failed to generalise to letters from a different hospital (Decker et al., 2022; Fernandes et al., 2024). LLMs were the first method to show strong generalisation to a new dataset (Gan et al., 2026), but by fine-tuning on over 1,000 synthetic letters. This paper asks whether similar performance is reachable with a prompted model and rules.

Rules were used to normalise frequencies and dates as a separate layer in two BERT-based pipelines (Xie et al., 2022b; Fernandes et al., 2024), and as minimal repair of malformed LLM output (Holgate et al., 2025), but no study has given rules a decision role after a modern LLM. This paper explores whether LLM instructions and examples combined with decision rules can match a fine-tuned model.

Clinical information extraction has been defined as the automatic extraction and encoding of clinical information from text: recognising a mention and normalising it into a standard form (Fu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2018). Modern LLMs do both in one step when asked for a normalised label such as *4 per month* from a raw statement such as *about once a week*. A further step exists in many clinical extraction pipelines but goes unmentioned: deciding between candidates. In a letter with several seizure-frequency statements, producing one label for the patient's current status requires the system both to extract the relevant statements and to decide which one answers the question. Prior rules-based systems made this a distinct stage, and clinical concept normalisation routinely separates high-recall candidate generation from a precise ranking stage (Xu et al., 2020). LLM pipelines instead make the decision inside the extraction prompt. This paper separates **extract** from **decide** and tests whether the decision, performed by rules or by a second LLM call on the same extraction record, improves on the provisional answer while preserving more of the evidence for downstream use.

Academic papers tend to focus on benchmark accuracy while practitioners value transparency, configurability and efficiency at the cost of some accuracy (Wang et al., 2018; Chiticariu et al., 2013). Interpretability is a critical factor in clinical settings (Fu et al., 2020). Past work uses evidence spans as a training input for fine-tuning (Gan et al., 2026) or as data exhaust of a rules-based method (Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2024). This paper preserves an evidence chain from exact quoted text, through a stated policy, to the final answer, and measures how far the accuracy depends on that evidence obligation.
