# Literature review draft

Clinical letters coordinate care, not research databases. They preserve observation, patient report, diagnostic judgement, treatment plans, uncertainty, temporal change, and context. Several statements may remain valid for different periods or phenomena, while a structured record requires explicit boundaries and values. Epilepsy-letter annotation studies report disagreement over spans and attributes even with detailed guidance and consensus review (Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2019, 2024). Clinical extraction therefore requires both locating relevant language and interpreting it for a defined research use.

Epilepsy, particularly seizure frequency, exposes this difficulty. Seizure frequency is a core clinical outcome measure used to describe current control and treatment response, and longitudinal frequency estimates support outcome research (Decker et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2023). A single letter may describe several seizure types, a historical baseline, a recent cluster, a seizure-free interval, and medication changes. Rates may be exact, ranged, vague, or hedged, and expressions such as “since last review” depend on temporal anchors elsewhere in the record. An extracted phrase may therefore be textually correct yet refer to the wrong seizure type or period.

Different research uses require different structured targets. The rules-led ExECT system builds a multi-fact inventory of diagnoses, seizure frequencies, prescriptions, and investigations with attributes and Unified Medical Language System mappings (Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2019, 2024). Xie et al. (2022) used classification and answer-span extraction; later work translated evidence into monthly values or current-frequency labels (Holgate et al., 2025; Gan et al., 2026). Together, these forms approximate complementary research information: a broad phenotype for cohort description and review, and seizure burden or current control for studies of treatment response and longer-term outcomes (Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2019; Xie et al., 2023). They require three decisions: finding evidence-linked candidates, translating them into the required form, and deciding what enters the final single answer or multi-fact inventory.

Epilepsy NLP often contrasts manually written rules with learned methods, although hybrid systems are established (Yew et al., 2023). Rules are explicit and inspectable but depend on local wording; learned models accommodate varied narrative but are harder to constrain and inspect. ExECT combines rules with statistical and existing NLP components, yet seizure frequency was its weakest extraction category (Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2019). Later learned systems reported strong results on narrower frequency tasks (Xie et al., 2022; Holgate et al., 2025), but their targets and datasets differ from ExECT. The evidence suggests that learned methods are particularly suitable for interpreting frequency statements, not universally superior. Hybrids also give rules clinically consequential work: Xie et al. (2023) combines transformer extraction and neural summarisation with rules-based quantification, while Fernandes et al. (2024) maps transformer-selected text to defined frequency and last-seizure categories using regular expressions.

Many reviewed evaluations use local, usually private corpora from recurring specialist centres, leaving cross-institutional and document-style transfer uncertain (Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2019, 2024; Xie et al., 2022, 2023; Holgate et al., 2025; Gan et al., 2026). A rules-based seizure extractor's F1 fell from 0.82 at its development centre to 0.40 externally (Decker et al., 2022), although one result cannot establish a transfer hierarchy between rules and models. The present study uses two public epilepsy-letter datasets from different sources and with different target forms, permitting independent replication or re-evaluation. This design reduces dependence on private, single-site evidence but does not isolate institutional transfer because source, task definition, and annotation policy differ together.

The epilepsy studies reviewed here usually evaluate complete pipelines or final predictions, providing little direct evidence about which component should make each clinically consequential decision. The present study compares learned interpretation, written task rules, and their combinations at the three decisions above, while retaining quoted evidence and recorded transformations. Across a current-frequency task and a multi-fact inventory task, it tests whether this balance remains useful across different clinical extraction tasks without assuming one mixture will suit both.
