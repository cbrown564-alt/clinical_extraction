# Introduction draft

Clinical information extraction aims to convert the rich but unstructured content of health records into data that can support cohort identification, retrospective outcome analysis, and longitudinal patient timelines. In epilepsy, clinically important information such as seizure frequency, treatment changes, diagnoses, and investigations is often recorded in narrative clinic letters rather than in consistently structured fields. Extracting this information by manual review is valuable but difficult to scale. The resulting challenge is therefore not to replace clinical judgement or to deliver a downstream application, but to make relevant information from narrative letters available in a structured form. **[Insert citations on epilepsy clinical narratives and downstream uses of clinical information extraction.]**

This is more than a problem of recognising medical terms. A single letter can contain several supported statements that differ in time, certainty, specificity, and clinical relevance. A structured output must nevertheless decide what to retain, what to treat as current or historical, how to handle uncertainty, and which representation to use. These are task-specific choices: there is no complete, task-neutral structured rendering of a clinical letter. Every extraction system therefore applies a representation policy, either explicitly through its design or implicitly through annotation guidance, prompts, and model behaviour. **[Insert citations on temporality, assertion, uncertainty, and annotation difficulty in clinical information extraction.]**

Existing approaches provide different forms of control. Rule-based systems can enforce controlled vocabularies and explicit representation constraints, but can be brittle when wording varies or relevant context is distributed across a narrative. Language models can interpret paraphrase, temporal context, and relations more flexibly, yet their proposed outputs still need to be reconciled with the requirements of a particular structured task. Hybrid clinical systems already combine models with retrieval, schema constraints, rule guidance, validation, and review. Their important design question is not simply whether rules are included, but where they enter the pipeline and what authority they have to influence, validate, or change a model-proposed output. **[Insert citations to epilepsy extraction systems, clinical hybrid systems, and neuro-symbolic integration work.]**

This study evaluates an evidence-backed hybrid workflow for extracting structured information from epilepsy clinic letters across two independently developed public benchmarks. The workflow asks language models to produce evidence-linked candidate information, then applies deterministic task policy while recording the transformations to the final output. The benchmarks impose complementary demands: one requires the selection and rendering of a current seizure-frequency representation, while the other requires the construction of a supported inventory across several clinical fact families. Rules-only, model-only, and intermediate configurations are compared separately for each task. The study therefore examines both agreement with benchmark representations and the role of explicit rule assistance in producing constrained, evidence-linked, reviewable outputs.
