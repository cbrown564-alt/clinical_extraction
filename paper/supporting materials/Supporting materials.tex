\subsection{Rules, BERT and LLMs Results Comparison}

A number of methods have shown performance at 
or above human level. Fonferko-Shadrach \emph{et al.} 
reported the annotators found annotation difficult 
and seizure frequency was the most difficult. This was 
reflected in the inter annotator agreement (IAA) score
of 0.47 (moderate agreement) even after multiple phases 
of development of the guidelines to meet the annotators' 
needs.  Xie \emph{et al.} reported an overall pairwise F1
score of 0.44 on a task focused extracting seizure 
freedom, frequency and most recent seizure
statements and recommend double or triple annotation. 
This is a long-standing challenge in the field, as Grisham 
and Sundheim noted in 1996 ' it proved remarkably difficult
to formulate guidelines which were reasonably complete 
and consistent'. Despite levels of IAA of 0.5, the literature 
commonly reports that rules-based and LLM-based 
methods achieving F1 scores of 0.8 and above. 



\subsection{Difference between Bert and LLMs}

Both BERT and LLMs are 
based on the same underlying transformers architecture, but
LLMs are distinguished primarily by the fact they are trained
on massive datasets with massive parameter counts which 
led to the emergent capability of in-context learning\cite{b11}.
This capability has been shown to transfer to clinical information
extraction among many other domains, and it has been shown
that giving the LLM clear instructions and examples can replace
the need for complex post-processing\cite{b12}. This paper 
explores whether LLM instructions and examples can be 
combined with complex post-processing to replace the need 
for fine-tuning.

\subsection{Modular Pipelines}

For this seizure frequency task, determining which one fact 
among many is correct is the challenge. Sometimes that 
requires grouping together multiple related facts into a 
single answer, e.g. '1 in June, 0 in July, 2 in August' 
becomes '3 in 3 months'. Sometimes it requires 
determining which fact takes precedence when there are 
multiple competing facts with varying degrees of specificity 
and certainty e.g. 'She may remain seizure-free for up to 
4 month, but then will experience clusters of 5 seizures in 
a single day'. Different annotation guidelines would record 
ambiguous facts like these differently. Distinct stages in the
pipeline make those choices configurable and explicit.

This complexity is a challenge for human annotators 
also; Fonferko-Shadrach \emph{et al.} described seizure
frequency as the most difficult category to annotate
and their inter-annotator agreement of 0.47 shows 
annotators often disagreed about how to represent
seizure frequency patterns\cite{b4}.

\subsection{Planning}

There are two key documents in the planning 
phase: 

\begin{enumerate}
\item The project brief, created by my supervisor.
\item The problem specification and project plan,

The project brief outlines the initial scope of the
project. The problem specification and project
plan refines the scope, specifies the research 
objectives and describes the methods used to
achieve them. The project brief was:

\begin{quote}
Instead of training models, this project builds a 
training-free (or minimal-training) multi-agent 
extraction system that reads epilepsy letters and 
outputs structured fields (e.g., ASM, seizure 
type/frequency, investigations, epilepsy 
syndrome/type). The student will design roles 
such as: (a) Section/Timeline Agent (segments

reliability: compare single-prompt extraction 
vs multi-agent extraction under the same 
budget constraints, and test improvements 
from self-consistency, evidence requirements 
(“answer only if supported by quote”), and 
structured output validation. Evaluation uses 
field-level accuracy/F1,  and robustness tests. 
Data can be synthetic or de-identified samples 
if available. Deliverables: an end-to-end agentic 
pipeline, evaluation harness, and a dissertation 
focused on engineering + empirical study of 
reliability.
\end{quote}

The deliverables are a narrow specification of
what a successful project would look like:

\begin{enumerate}
\item An end-to-end agentic pipeline.
\item An evaluation harness.
\item A dissertation focused on engineering + empirical study of reliability.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Development}

This section explains how the initial project proposal was 
translated into a focused and testable research approach. 
It records the evidence, design choices, and trade-offs 
that led to the final method.

\subsubsection{Original Design Requirements}

The original brief contained a series of key design 
requirements for the pipeline. It must:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Ingest epilepsy letters
\item Output validated structured fields 
\item Contain verified evidence citations
\item Produce this in an aggregated JSON
\end{enumerate}

These design requirements were tightly coupled to a 
multi-agent framework, e.g using a \emph{Verification Agent} 
to check evidence spans and an \emph{Aggregator Agent}
to produce the final JSON and citations. 

The literature review showed that multi-agent systems
are particularly useful for complex tasks \cite{b21} and
they particularly on long-context tasks \cite{b22}. 
However, research also shows that single-agent systems
are as effective when they are given equal compute 
and multi-agent systems create new failure modes 
as errors propagate throughout the system\cite{b22}.

For this task, the full epilepsy letter and instructions 
fit comfortably in the LLM's context window so there
is no need for convoluted multi-hop pipelines or 
document splitting that multi-agent systems 
performed particularly well on\cite{b19}. Thus, a 
multi-agent system can not be justified on that basis.

It is less clear whether this task is sufficiently complex
to justify a multi-agent system. Past research has 
shown that LLMs struggle with temporal logic and 
numerical logic, and both occur in this task. It is
also a task that human annotators struggle to do
reliably and consistently. This suggests the task 
is at least somewhat complex and a multi-agent
system is justified on that basis.

However, it is important that the each agent is given
a sufficiently complex task to justify the added system
complexity. A key goal of the research is to design 
a reliable system. A reliable system is one that 
contains the minimum amount of complexity 
necessary to function. More components means 
creates more failure modes, and in a sequential 
process those errors propagate and multiply at 
each step. 

\subsubsection{Deterministic Code for Deterministic Tasks}

A key design choice early on was to simplify. Any
task that can be reliably completed by a deterministic
system (code) instead of a probabilistic system (LLMs)
should be done deterministically. 

Evidence verification and schema validation are two 
tasks that code can do reliably and efficiently. Initial 
experiments showed that LLMs can not do this task 
reliably; they introduce as many new schema errors 
as they repair, and they occasionally miss invalid 
evidence. However even if they could do the task
reliably, code already does this at a much lower
cost and latency. 

Therefore a dedicated Aggregator Agent to produce
the final JSON with text citations and a dedicated 
Verification Agent to check evidence spans were 
removed from the design after initial experiments.
A Field Extractor agent was no longer relevant as 
the task was limited to a single field, seizure 
frequency. Initial experiments showed that a 
Section/Timeline agent was a net-negative 
contribution because correct seizure frequency 
references were not always in the dedicated 
seizure frequency section, and splitting up the 
text encouraged the LLM to select wrong answers
more frequently.

\subsubsection{Pipeline Design Review}

After initial experiments, all four of the original
proposed LLM agents had been ruled out because
they were now either invalid or empirically performed
worse than a single LLM. This led to a major review
of the pipeline design. 

Comprehensive error analysis showed that the LLM
was consistently finding valid seizure frequency
patterns, providing exact evidence from the text
and often choosing defensible answers based 
on the instructions. It did not another agent to
check its evidence or structure it into JSON, it 
needed cleaner task separation. 

The task of extracting a single seizure frequency
label is composed of three sub-tasks: finding 
relevant mentions of seizure frequency in the 
text, encoding that information into the form 
the gold-standard labels expect, and applying the
correct policy to choose which of those encoded
labels is the correct seizure frequency pattern.

The original multi-agent pipeline separated
mechanical tasks across multiple LLMs; one
agent splits the evidence, another checks
the evidence, another aggregates it together.
It would be preferable if a single LLM could
do all of those tasks at once. This would require
the LLM to generate a much richer intermediate
schema. Rather than extracting a single 
seizure frequency label, the LLM would extract
every relevant seizure frequency pattern as 
a distinct entity in a nested JSON, with exact
evidence for every entity, make an initial 
proposal. 

Using this richer schema, a subsequent LLM 
could then review the evidence and apply the correct 
policy in hard cases. This would reframe the
multi-agent pipeline into separate agents for
separate clinical tasks instead of mechanical
tasks. This richer schema and three stage 
pipeline also makes it possible for deterministic
rules to apply policies to the evidence generated
by the LLM to select the final answer.

Past research shows that deterministic code
can reliably normalise seizure frequency values,
and hybrid LLM and rules systems can outperform
LLMs alone. Rules-based systems 
struggled with seizure frequency extraction more
than medications, but they still performed better
than human experts. Deterministic code already
performed schema validation and evidence 
verification more reliably than LLMs, so maybe
there was a role for more deterministic code in 
the pipeline.

\subsubsection{Final Pipeline Design}

The final outcome of this design review was 
a modular pipeline that could use either LLMs
or rules at each of the three three stages 
\texttt{find}, \texttt{encode} and \texttt{select}. 
Three methods would be implemented:
a multi-agent pipeline, a purely rules-based system 
and a hybrid LLM and rules system. 

The original project brief required a pipeline that
could ingest epilepsy letters, output validated 
structured fields, contain verified evidence citations
and produce this in an aggregated JSON. It required
an evaluation harness and a dissertation focused 
on engineering and an empirical study of reliability.
This design meets all of these requirements. 

The three method comparison strengthens the 
empirical core and deepens the focus on reliability 
by using the evaluation harness to determine
whether a multi-agent pipeline is the most reliable 
pipeline.

\subsection{Implementation}

This section describes the system that implemented 
the development decisions introduced above. The 
implementation was designed to produce a structured, 
reproducible account of current seizure frequency from 
a clinical letter, while retaining the evidence and 
intermediate choices needed to inspect how an answer 
was reached. It describes the reusable pipeline and the 
controlled comparison design; performance results and 
the findings of individual ablations are reported in the 
evaluation section.

Each implementation receives a synthetic clinical letter 
and produces a structured seizure frequency record. The 
final record contains the submitted frequency label, direct 
supporting evidence, and the metadata needed to identify 
the method, model configuration, data split, and scoring 
policy used. This design makes the final answer usable for 
benchmark evaluation without reducing the whole process 
to an opaque single-label extraction.

The same overall record structure is used across the 
implemented approaches. Rules-only, LLM-only, and hybrid 
methods differ in who performs particular clinical extraction 
stages, rather than in the basic representation of a case or 
the way outputs are stored and checked. This common 
structure makes it possible to compare methods while 
preserving the intermediate evidence required for replay 
and inspection.

The pipeline uses a richer intermediate record than the 
final benchmark label. During extraction, it can retain 
multiple candidate seizure frequency events, their source 
spans, raw wording, time information, and an initial 
proposed answer. This is important because a letter 
may contain several plausible statements: historical 
and current rates, periods without seizures, isolated 
breakthrough events, clusters, and uncertain or 
qualitative descriptions. Preserving the alternatives avoids 
losing information before the annotation policy has been 
applied.

The record distinguishes the source wording from its 
encoded representation and from the final selected 
answer. A later selection stage can revise the proposed 
answer while retaining the evidence on which 
the original proposal was based. The final output is 
validated against the expected schema, while source 
data and raw model output are retained separately 
from format repair and clinical policy decisions.

The implemented pipeline divides seizure frequency 
extraction into three stages. \texttt{Find} collects candidate 
events and their evidence from the letter. \texttt{Encode} 
represents an already identified candidate in the required 
structured form, including the benchmark's label 
conventions. \texttt{Select} applies the policy for determining 
which candidate best represents the patient's current 
seizure frequency pattern. This division makes explicit 
that locating a relevant statement, writing it in the desired 
form, and choosing the correct final statement are 
different tasks.

Each stage produces an inspectable output. This is 
particularly useful when the selected answer changes: the change can 
be attributed to the evidence found, its encoding, or the 
final policy decision. It also permits later stages to work from 
the same saved candidate record, rather than requiring 
every alternative implementation to make a new model call.
This significantly sped up the optimisation process.

Deterministic components implement tasks for which explicit, 
reproducible handling is appropriate. These include parsing 
and schema repair, validation of required evidence fields, 
aggregation of the final record, and rules for normalising 
frequency expressions into the task's closed vocabulary. The 
implementation keeps format-only repair, benchmark 
representation, and clinically meaningful policy rules distinct 
so that a change to one does not silently alter another.

Rules also provide an implemented option at each 
substantive stage. They can identify seizure-frequency 
statements, encode candidate events, and apply the 
final selection policy. Their authority is recorded by rule 
type: for example, whether a rule merely standardises a 
representation, blocks an unsupported candidate, rewrites 
a submitted concept, or reselects between already found 
events. This makes deterministic processing an explicit 
part of the method rather than unreported post-processing.

The LLM implementation uses structured prompts that 
ask the model to return a record containing candidate 
seizure-frequency events, evidence, and a proposed 
answer. The prompt can preserve the original 
wording or request an output already expressed in the 
target vocabulary, depending on the implementation 
being examined. Structured parsing and validation then 
convert the response into the common intermediate 
record.

LLMs can be assigned to \texttt{find}, \texttt{encode}, \texttt{select}. 
At \texttt{find}, an LLM identifies candidate 
events and evidence. At \texttt{encode}, it maps an already 
represented candidate to the required label form. At \texttt{select}, 
it adjudicates between competing candidates under the 
same policy that deterministic rules may apply. This 
stage-assignment approach provides a controlled way to 
compare rules and LLMs on the substantive clinical extraction 
decisions, while deterministic validation and output assembly
remain common infrastructure.

\subsection{Testing}

Testing verified that the implemented system and research 
controls behaved as intended before its comparative results 
were interpreted.

Data-loading tests verify that source records, gold labels, 
evidence references, quality flags, and label normalisation 
are preserved. The test suite also checks that the fixed
split manifest contains the intended disjoint training, 
development, and held-out partitions, and that loaders 
return records in the manifest-defined order. These checks 
protect against accidental changes to the dataset surface, 
label interpretation, or the membership of a reported split.

The split policy was treated as a testable research safeguard 
rather than a documentation convention. Development work 
used the permitted development partition for prompt and rule 
changes, while the held-out partition could be used only under 
a fixed protocol and reported at aggregate level. Tests enforce 
that held-out replay artifacts do not retain row identifiers, source 
notes, labels, predictions, or error lists. 

Focused regression tests capture failure modes identified during 
development. These include aggregating diary entries across 
months, avoiding double-counting when the same evidence is 
repeated or split across candidates, handling zero-count months, 
interpreting clusters, and distinguishing current seizure burden 
from historical or less relevant statements. Each fixture represents 
a named clinical or annotation-policy obligation, rather than a 
generic collection of examples.These tests were used whenever 
a prompt, rule, parser, or stage boundary changed. Their purpose 
was to show that a local improvement did not silently regress 
previously correct behaviour. 

The implemented methods were tested as pipelines as well as 
individual functions. Prompt-contract tests check that model 
requests preserve the required structured fields and do not 
inadvertently introduce irrelevant metadata. Pipeline and runner 
tests check that the intended components run in the intended 
order and that their outputs are written into the common record 
structure.

Together, these tests verify that the implementation produces 
valid records, applies the intended data and split controls, and 
preserves known clinical-policy behaviour. 

\subsection{Evaluation}

This section evaluates whether the verified pipeline met the 
research objective: to extract a patient's current seizure 
frequency burden from a clinical letter reliably, with a 
structured evidence record and a reproducible comparison 
between rules and LLMs. 

The evaluation separates development evidence from 
held-out evidence. Development records can be used to 
inspect mechanisms, test a specific explanation, and trace 
how an implementation change affected a result. The locked 
hold-out is used only for aggregate comparisons after the 
relevant method, prompt, scorer, split, and row policy have 
been fixed. This distinction allows the section to report both 
how the pipeline works and how the final configured methods 
generalise.

Targeted ablations explain why preserving separate find, 
encode, and select stages is useful. A source-near LLM 
request can retain wording closer to the letter, but it incurs a 
lower immediate score because the benchmark expects a 
constrained label form. Later encoding and selection stages 
recover much of this gap. Conversely, asking an LLM to 
rewrite already extracted candidates in a separate later 
encoding call reduced performance in the reported ablation. 
These findings show that the location and responsibility of an 
encoding decision matter; adding another model call is 
not automatically beneficial.

A key feature of the pipeline was the rich schema. 
Models were asked to extract multiple seizure frequency
patterns in the text, extract evidence spans for each 
pattern identified, and choose which of those patterns
best represents the patient's current seizure frequency
status. That rich schema is what enabled a subsequent
LLM in the multi-agent method and rules in the hybrid
method to re-use the evidence and deliver performance
gains of +0.07. However that comes at the cost of higher
schema complexity and task specificity and this is where 
local models remain slightly worse. 

Gemini, Grok, Luna and DeepSeek all had exact evidence 
rates of 99\%+. Qwen (97\%) and Gemma (93\%) occasionally 
paraphrased the text. Gemini, Grok and Luna produced valid
schema 100\% of the time. DeepSeek and Qwen produced
valid schema 99\% of the time. Gemma was only at 97\%, 
occasionally using things like double \{\{ or "" and breaking
the nested json structure. 

Comprehensive error analysis showed that the LLM
was consistently choosing valid answers based 
on the instructions. For example, the LLM is told
to choose the seizure frequency with the highest
current or recent seizure pattern if multiple seizure 
events are available. When it sees a pattern such
as \emph{2 in Jan, 0 in Feb, 0 in March, 5 in April}
the model chooses 5 because it is the largest 
and most recent pattern. However the gold-standard
label is 7 in 4 months. When the prompt instruction
is changed to add up all seizure events in a period,
then it combines tonic-clonic seizures and generalised
seizures over overlapping periods, while the gold-standard
choose the highest count among the two seizure types.
Separating these complex, somewhat contradictory 
instructions into a separate LLM prompt at the select
phase produced significant improvements (+0.06).

\section{Gan codebook find prompt}

The cited language-model find request is the codebook 
extract prompt (\texttt{gan\_llm\_extract}). The object below is 
the frozen template sent to the model: task, instructions, 
allowed label forms, event schema, and selection schema. 
The letter is attached at run time as \texttt{note\_text} and is 
omitted here. Research metadata such as 
\texttt{prompt\_version} is not part of the model-facing 
payload.

This template is not the source-near find ablation 
(\texttt{gan\_llm\_extract\_raw}), which has no closed 
\texttt{label\_forms} block. It is not the later select prompt.


The only preprocessing applied to the answer labels 
was to use the CUIPhrase as the main answer key
for diagnosis and seizure frequency instead of the 
raw text value. Often they were the same label, 
but the CUIPhrase sometimes included cleaner labels
for matching answers e.g. the normalised CUIPhrase 
'temporal-lobe-epilepsy' was used over the text
'epilepsy-Probable-temporal' for the same span.
