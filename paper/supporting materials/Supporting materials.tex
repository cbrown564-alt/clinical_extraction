\section{Extended Literature Review}

\subsection{Complexity of Clinical Narratives}

Clinical letters use narrative language so that clinicians can 
record the patient's situation, their judgement, and their 
uncertainty. That flexibility supports clinical communication, 
but it makes the information difficult to use at scale~\cite{b1}. 
Clinicians often use hedging to describe certainty, frequency, 
and quantity rather than stating a precise value~\cite{b2}. 
The task of information extraction (IE) is to convert that
nuanced information into a standardised form while
preserving its essential meaning.

Epilepsy clinic letters illustrate that complexity. One letter 
can contain diagnoses, prescriptions, history, seizure 
descriptions, and several references to seizure frequency. 
Fonferko-Shadrach \emph{et al.} report much lower human 
agreement for seizure frequency (per-item F1 = 0.47) than 
for prescriptions (0.87) or diagnoses (0.83)\cite{b3}. They 
attribute this to the variety of frequency expressions and to 
mentions of more than one seizure or event type. 
Effective extraction requires clearly defined policies and
procedures describing what to extract and how to 
represent it.

\subsection{Seizure Frequency as a Selection Task}

The same letter can be used for different purposes. 
ExECT and the later annotation study treat it as 
an inventory: they record several entity mentions and 
their attributes. One letter may list several anti-seizure 
medications, each with a name, dose, unit, and 
administration frequency~\cite{b4,b3}. That form is 
appropriate when the aim is building a broad clinical
inventory.

The task in this study has a different purpose. Gan \emph{et al.} 
assign each letter one label for the patient's current 
seizure frequency status~\cite{b5}. The system has to gather 
candidate descriptions, interpret their meaning and 
time frame, and choose the single normalised label 
that best represents the current pattern. Earlier 
one-label studies use the same formulation~\cite{b6,b7,b8}. 
The single label extraction implies that it is all one 
task, but this paper proposes two separate stages: 
extracting every candidate with its evidence in a 
canonical form, then deciding the final answer from 
those candidates.

Two common cases show why that split matters. 
Diary-style counts may need to be combined: \emph{1 in June, 
0 in July, 2 in August} can become \texttt{3 in 3 months}. 
Competing statements may instead require precedence: 
\emph{She may remain seizure-free for up to 4 months, but 
then will experience clusters of 5 seizures in a single day.} 
Different annotation guidelines would record those cases 
differently. Separate stages make the policy choice 
inspectable rather than implicit.

\subsection{Rules, Fine-Tuned Models, and LLMs}

Several papers have published seizure frequency 
extraction systems using rules, fine-tuned models, or 
prompted large language models. Rules match terms 
and apply domain logic. ExECT does this for a clinical 
inventory; Decker \emph{et al.} use regular expressions 
for seizure-related variables~\cite{b4,b9}. Fine-tuned 
BERT models learn the mapping from labelled examples, 
as in Xie \emph{et al.} and Fernandes \emph{et al.}~\cite{b6,b10}. 
Table~\ref{tab:historical} lists the F1 scores those studies 
report. The scores are not a ranking of methods: each 
study uses its own dataset, label set, and evaluation.

Prompted and fine-tuned LLMs are more recent. 
Holgate \emph{et al.} show that a frozen model can 
extract seizure information from instructions and 
examples~\cite{b7}. Later work, including Holgate \emph{et al.} 
and Gan \emph{et al.}, fine-tunes a model to a defined 
output format~\cite{b8,b5}. 

BERT-style encoders and decoder-based LLMs 
share the transformer architecture. The practical 
difference is in-context learning: a LLM can 
follow instructions and examples without a 
task-specific training run~\cite{b11}. That behaviour 
transfers to clinical extraction, where clear 
instructions and examples can reduce the need for 
heavy post-processing~\cite{b12}. Gan \emph{et al.}
show that combining structured prompting and 
fine-tuning can deliver even stronger performance. 
This paper explores whether structured
prompting in a modular system can deliver 
comparable performance to a fine-tuned LLM.

\subsection{Modular and Sequential Extraction Pipelines}

Multi-stage clinical extraction pipelines split a task 
into distinct operations instead of asking a model for 
one final answer in a single step. CLINES uses 
stages for chunking, extraction, attribute assignment, 
normalisation, date resolution, and aggregation~\cite{b14}. 
Seizure frequency studies have also decomposed 
the task in structured prompting including numbered 
reasoning steps and summarise-then-extract 
designs~\cite{b7,b15}. 

This study decomposes the task into two stages: 
extracting candidate evidence into a structured 
record, and deciding the final answer from that 
record. One LLM call performs the extraction. The 
decision stage is performed in two ways on the same 
saved record, by recorded rules and by a second LLM 
call, so the two executors can be compared with the 
extraction held fixed. Past research on seizure 
frequency gives rules only a repair role after an 
LLM; this paper gives them the decision role.

\section{Development}

This section explains how the initial project proposal was 
translated into a focused and testable research approach. 
It records the evidence, design choices, and trade-offs 
that led to the final method.

\subsection{Original Design Requirements}

The original brief contained a series of key design 
requirements for the pipeline. It must:

\vspace{0.2em}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Ingest epilepsy letters
\item Output validated structured fields 
\item Contain verified evidence citations
\item Produce this in an aggregated JSON
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{0.2em}

These design requirements were tightly coupled to a 
multi-agent framework, e.g using a \emph{Verification Agent} 
to check evidence spans and an \emph{Aggregator Agent}
to produce the final JSON and citations. 

The literature review showed that multi-agent systems
are particularly useful for complex tasks \cite{b25},
especially on long-context tasks \cite{b26}. 
However, research also shows that single-agent systems
are often as effective when they are given equal compute 
and multi-agent systems create new failure modes 
as errors propagate throughout the system\cite{b26}.

For this task, the full epilepsy letter and instructions 
fit comfortably in the LLM's context window so there
is no need for convoluted multi-hop pipelines or 
document splitting that multi-agent systems 
performed particularly well on\cite{b14}. Thus, a 
multi-agent system can not be justified on that basis.

It is less clear whether this task is sufficiently complex
to justify a multi-agent system. Past research has 
shown that LLMs struggle with temporal logic and 
numerical logic, and both occur in this task. It is
also a task that human annotators struggle to do
reliably and consistently\cite{b3}. This suggests the task 
is at least somewhat complex and a multi-agent
system is justified on that basis.

However, it is important that each LLM call is given
a sufficiently complex task to justify the added system
complexity. A key goal of the research is to design 
a reliable system. A reliable system is one that 
contains the minimum amount of complexity 
necessary to function. More components means 
creates more failure modes, and in a sequential 
process those errors propagate and multiply at 
each step. 

\subsection{Deterministic Code for Deterministic Tasks}

A key design choice early on was to simplify. Any
task that can be reliably completed by a deterministic
system (code) instead of a probabilistic system (LLMs)
should be done deterministically. 

Evidence verification and schema validation are two 
tasks that code can do reliably and efficiently. Initial 
experiments showed that LLMs can not do this task 
reliably; they introduce as many new schema errors 
as they repair, and they occasionally miss invalid 
evidence. However even if they could do the task
reliably, code already does this at a much lower
cost and latency. 

Therefore a dedicated \emph{Aggregator Agent} to produce
the final JSON with text citations and a dedicated 
\emph{Verification Agent} to check evidence spans were 
removed from the design after initial experiments.
A \emph{Field Extractor} agent was no longer relevant as 
the task was limited to a single field, seizure 
frequency. Initial experiments showed that a 
\emph{Section/Timeline Agent} was a net-negative 
contribution because correct seizure frequency 
references were not always in the dedicated 
seizure frequency section, and splitting up the 
text encouraged the LLM to select wrong answers
more frequently.

\subsection{Pipeline Design Review}

After initial experiments, all four of the original
proposed LLM agents had been ruled out because
they were now either invalid or empirically performed
worse than a single LLM. This led to a major review
of the pipeline design. 

Comprehensive error analysis showed that the LLM
was consistently finding valid seizure frequency
patterns, providing exact evidence from the text
and often choosing defensible answers based 
on the instructions. It did not need another LLM call to
check its evidence or structure it into JSON; it 
needed cleaner task separation. 

The task of extracting the correct seizure frequency
label is composed of three sub-tasks: finding 
relevant mentions of seizure frequency in the 
text, encoding that information into the form 
the gold-standard labels expect, and applying a
policy to choose which of those labels matches the 
gold-standard selected label. The
labelling conventions and policy choices in the 
gold-standard evidence are valid, but they are not
the only valid choice. Therefore a key pipeline 
requirement is producing results that conform to 
an explicit set of subjective choices. 

The original brief separated mechanical tasks 
across multiple LLM calls; one call splits the 
evidence, another checks the evidence, another 
aggregates it together. With simplicity and 
efficiency in mind, it would be preferable if a 
single LLM call could do all of those tasks at once. 
This would require the LLM to generate a much richer 
intermediate schema. Rather than extracting a single 
seizure frequency label, the LLM would extract
every relevant seizure frequency pattern as 
a distinct entity in a nested JSON, with exact
evidence for every entity, and make an initial 
proposal. 

Using this richer schema, a second LLM call 
could then review the evidence and apply the correct 
policy in hard cases. This reframes the decomposition 
around clinical tasks (extract the candidates, then 
decide between them) instead of mechanical tasks. The 
richer record also makes it possible for deterministic 
rules to apply the same policy to the evidence the LLM 
extracted.

Past research shows that deterministic code
can reliably normalise seizure frequency values,
and hybrid LLM and rules systems can outperform
LLMs alone. Rules-based systems 
struggled with seizure frequency extraction more
than medications, but they still performed better
than human experts. Deterministic code already
performed schema validation and evidence 
verification more reliably than LLMs, so there was 
a case for more deterministic code in the pipeline.

\subsection{Final Pipeline Design}

The final outcome of this design review was a 
pipeline with two paper-facing stages, 
\texttt{extract} and \texttt{decide}. The 
implementation names three stages, \texttt{find}, 
\texttt{encode} and \texttt{select}: the cited 
extraction prompt already writes labels in the 
gold standard's form, so \texttt{find} and 
\texttt{encode} are bundled into one call and 
reported as \texttt{extract}; \texttt{select} is 
reported as \texttt{decide}. Those implementation 
names are kept here only where they identify an 
existing artifact.

The paper's core comparison holds the extraction call 
fixed and changes only who performs \texttt{decide}: 
recorded rules (\texttt{Hybrid}) or a second LLM call 
(\texttt{LLM-only}). Because the decision stage reads 
the saved record, both executors replay the same model 
output without a new call, and every difference between 
them is attributable to the decision stage. 

During development, rules were also implemented for 
the extraction role, and an end-to-end rules-only 
baseline was built and scored. That baseline is not 
part of the dissertation comparison. It remains in the 
repository as development history with its own recorded 
protocol; it is not reinterpreted here. The other 
decompositions of the LLM work that were tried are 
summarised in the Evaluation section as secondary 
experiments with their evidence retained.

The original project brief required a pipeline that
could ingest epilepsy letters, output validated 
structured fields, contain verified evidence citations
and produce this in an aggregated JSON. It required
an evaluation harness and a dissertation focused 
on engineering and an empirical study of reliability.
Each requirement maps onto a mechanism and its 
evidence: validated structured fields to the 
schema-validated record (Table~\ref{tab:adherence-supp}); 
verified evidence citations to the exact-quote obligation 
and the exact-evidence rate; aggregated JSON to the 
final record; and the empirical study of reliability to 
the two-executor comparison, the prompt ablations, the 
six-model comparison and the sampling ablations reported 
in the paper.

The two-executor comparison keeps the empirical core 
on the brief's question. It asks whether a second LLM 
call is needed to apply the policy reliably, or whether 
recorded code does the same work from the same record, 
and it answers with a paired test on held-out letters.

\section{Implementation}

The implementation was designed to produce a structured, 
reproducible account of current seizure frequency from 
a clinical letter, while retaining the evidence and 
intermediate choices needed to inspect how an answer 
was reached. It describes the reusable pipeline and the 
controlled comparison design; performance results and 
the findings of individual ablations are reported in the 
evaluation section.

\subsection{Structured Record}

Each configuration receives a synthetic clinical letter 
and produces a structured seizure frequency record. The 
final record contains the submitted frequency label, direct 
supporting evidence, and the metadata needed to identify 
the method, model configuration, data split, and scoring 
policy used. This design makes the final answer usable for 
benchmark evaluation without reducing the whole process 
to an opaque single-label extraction.

The pipeline uses a richer intermediate record than the 
final benchmark label. During extraction, it can retain 
multiple candidate seizure frequency events, their source 
spans, raw wording, time information, and an initial 
proposed answer. This is important because a letter 
may contain several plausible statements: historical 
and current rates, periods without seizures, isolated 
breakthrough events, clusters, and uncertain or 
qualitative descriptions. Preserving the alternatives avoids 
losing information before the annotation policy has been 
applied.

\subsection{Extract and Decide}

The record distinguishes the source wording from its 
encoded representation and from the final selected 
answer. The decision stage can revise the provisional 
answer while retaining the evidence on which 
the original proposal was based. The final output is 
validated against the expected schema, while source 
data and raw model output are retained separately 
from format repair and clinical policy decisions.

The interface between the two stages is fixed. 
\texttt{Extract} receives the full letter and returns the 
candidate record: every seizure frequency event with its 
quoted span, category and canonical label, plus a 
provisional answer. \texttt{Decide} receives that record 
and the decision policy, never the letter, and returns the 
final label with the event(s) that support it. In the 
implementation, \texttt{extract} is the \texttt{find} stage 
whose prompt already writes the gold standard's label 
forms (so the \texttt{encode} step is bundled into the same 
call), and \texttt{decide} is the \texttt{select} stage. A 
small deterministic \texttt{encode} step still runs 
between them in \texttt{Hybrid} to rewrite any label the 
model left in a non-canonical form; on the held-out set it 
changes at most 0.01 (355 to 360 correct letters before 
rules decide).

Each stage produces an inspectable output. This is 
particularly useful when the final answer differs from 
the provisional one: the change can be attributed to the 
evidence extracted or to the policy decision. It also 
permits the decision stage to work from the same saved 
candidate record, rather than requiring every alternative 
implementation to make a new model call. This 
significantly sped up the optimisation process and is 
what makes the two-executor comparison a paired one.

\subsection{Rules and Deterministic Components}

Deterministic components implement tasks for which explicit, 
reproducible handling is appropriate. These include parsing 
and schema repair, validation of required evidence fields, 
aggregation of the final record, and rules for normalising 
frequency expressions into the task's closed vocabulary. The 
implementation keeps format-only repair, benchmark 
representation, and clinically meaningful policy rules distinct 
so that a change to one does not silently alter another.

In \texttt{Hybrid}, recorded rules perform the decision 
role. Their authority is recorded by rule type: whether a 
rule merely standardises a representation (dialect or 
encode), blocks an unsupported provisional answer (gate), 
rewrites the submitted label from the extracted evidence 
(rewrite, e.g. totalling a seizure diary), or reselects a 
different already-extracted event (reselect). No decision 
rule scans the letter for a new candidate. This makes 
deterministic processing an explicit part of the method 
rather than unreported post-processing. Rules for the 
extraction role were also implemented during development; 
they are repository history, not part of the reported 
comparison.

\subsection{LLM Components}

The LLM implementation uses structured prompts that 
ask the model to return a record containing candidate 
seizure-frequency events, evidence, and a proposed 
answer. The extraction call reads the letter and returns 
that record. In \texttt{LLM-only}, a second call receives 
the record and adjudicates between competing candidates 
under the same policy that the rules apply, written as 
instructions with worked examples. Neither call uses 
tools, chooses the next step or invokes another model; 
each is a prompt with a fixed input and a schema-validated 
JSON output, which is why the system is described as a 
fixed pipeline of LLM calls rather than as agents.

Holding the extraction call fixed and changing only the 
decision executor provides a controlled way to compare 
rules and LLMs on the substantive clinical decision, while 
deterministic validation and output assembly remain common 
infrastructure.

\section{Testing}

Testing verified that the implemented system and research 
controls behaved as intended before its comparative results 
were interpreted.

Data-loading tests verify that source records, gold labels, 
evidence references, quality flags, and label normalisation 
are preserved. The test suite also checks that the fixed
split manifest contains the intended disjoint training, 
development, and held-out partitions, and that loaders 
return records in the manifest-defined order. These checks 
protect against accidental changes to the dataset surface, 
label interpretation, or the membership of a reported split.

The split policy was treated as a testable research safeguard 
rather than a documentation convention. Development work 
used the permitted development partition for prompt and rule 
changes, while the held-out partition could be used only under 
a fixed protocol and reported at aggregate level. Tests enforce 
that held-out replay artifacts do not retain row identifiers, source 
notes, labels, predictions, or error lists. 

Focused regression tests capture failure modes identified during 
development. These include aggregating diary entries across 
months, avoiding double-counting when the same evidence is 
repeated or split across candidates, handling zero-count months, 
interpreting clusters, and distinguishing current seizure burden 
from historical or less relevant statements. Each fixture represents 
a named clinical or annotation-policy obligation, rather than a 
generic collection of examples.These tests were used whenever 
a prompt, rule, parser, or stage boundary changed. Their purpose 
was to show that a local improvement did not silently regress 
previously correct behaviour. 

The implemented methods were tested as pipelines as well as 
individual functions. Prompt-contract tests check that model 
requests preserve the required structured fields and do not 
inadvertently introduce irrelevant metadata. Pipeline and runner 
tests check that the intended components run in the intended 
order and that their outputs are written into the common record 
structure.

Together, these tests verify that the implementation produces 
valid records, applies the intended data and split controls, and 
preserves known clinical-policy behaviour. 

\section{Evaluation}

This section evaluates whether the verified pipeline met the 
research objective: to extract a patient's current seizure 
frequency burden from a clinical letter reliably, with a 
structured evidence record and a reproducible comparison 
between rules and LLMs. 

All held-out (\texttt{test450}) figures in this section are 
aggregate totals; no held-out letter or output was inspected. 
Development (\texttt{dev750}) figures may be accompanied by 
row-level mechanism examples. Unless stated otherwise the 
model is Gemini 3.7 Flash at temperature 0 with low thinking 
effort, and the metric is Purist micro-F1 with correct letters 
in parentheses.

\subsection{Extracting Multiple Candidates with Exact Evidence}

A key feature of the pipeline is the rich extraction record. 
The model is asked to extract every seizure frequency pattern 
in the text, quote an exact evidence span for each, write each 
in a canonical form, and propose which best represents the 
patient's current status. That record is what allows the 
second LLM call in \texttt{LLM-only} and the rules in 
\texttt{Hybrid} to re-use the evidence and improve on the 
provisional answer by +0.06 to +0.07. The cost is a more 
complex schema, and this is where the local models fall 
slightly behind.

The gold standard records one label and one quote per letter. 
The extraction record holds more: on \texttt{test450}, Gemini 
extracted 985 events over 450 letters (2.19 per letter) and 
the six models ranged from 2.19 to 2.82 events per letter. On 
\texttt{dev750} the Gemini mean is 2.16 events (median 2, 
range 1--6), while the set of events supporting the final 
answer has a median of 1. The record therefore preserves 
alternatives that a one-label output would discard.

Table~\ref{tab:adherence-supp} gives each model's adherence 
to the extraction contract on \texttt{test450}. Every model 
returned a parseable record for at least 442 of 450 letters 
and no call failed. The four hosted models quoted an exact 
substring of the letter for the selected event in 99\% or more 
of letters; Qwen (97\%) and Gemma (93\%) occasionally 
paraphrased. Gemma also needed the most non-semantic format 
repair (15 letters), typically doubled braces or quotation 
marks that broke the nested JSON, and left 8 letters 
unparsed after repair; those letters are scored as incorrect. 
Format repair is recorded separately from every semantic 
rule and never changes a label.

\subsection{Extraction Prompt Variants}

\subsection{Secondary Decompositions of the LLM Work}

The brief asked whether more LLM calls make extraction more 
reliable. The following decompositions were built and 
measured. None replaces the two-stage design and they are 
reported here, with their scope, so that the negative and 
null results are on record.

\emph{A separate label-rewriting call.} Inserting a second 
LLM call that rewrites each extracted event into the gold 
form, before a third call decides, made the score worse. On 
\texttt{test450} the codebook extraction stop of 0.79 (354) 
fell to 0.65 (291) after the rewriting call and recovered 
only to 0.71 (320) after the deciding call, with the 
development split showing the same shape (0.78, 0.67, 0.76). 
The development mechanism study found 89 harmed and 21 
rescued letters at the rewriting call. A later replay with 
the living decision prompt confirmed the drop (0.68, 304) and 
showed the living decide call recovering to 0.86 (386), one 
letter below \texttt{Hybrid}. The rewriting call does not 
re-read the letter and overwrote answers that were already 
correct. This is why \texttt{LLM-only} is two calls, not 
three.

\emph{One call for everything.} After the main comparison 
was fixed, a single prompt that states the decision policy 
and its worked examples inside the extraction call was run 
as an ablation. Scored at its own provisional answer, it 
reaches 0.87 (392) on \texttt{test450} and 0.88 (657) on 
\texttt{dev750}, one letter above \texttt{Hybrid} on 
development and five above on holdout, with no parse or 
call failures. It is reported as an ablation, not as a 
headline row: it was not part of the predeclared comparison 
and has not been promoted. It shows that the policy can also 
be applied inside the extraction call. The case for the 
separate decision stage is not a score margin; it is that 
the decision is inspectable, replayable without a model call, 
and changeable without re-prompting.

\emph{Verification then correction.} A development-only 
variant asked a second call to verify the provisional label 
against its quoted evidence and correct it. It removed true 
positives, because the gold label is a labelling convention 
rather than a paraphrase of the quote (\emph{multiple per 
week} against \emph{many events in the last week}). A related 
generate-then-verify program optimised with DSPy did not beat 
the development ceiling of the time (0.731). No clean 
held-out artifact exists for this variant.

\emph{Section or timeline splitting.} A development-only 
variant split the letter into sections before extraction. It 
was a net negative: the correct seizure frequency reference 
was not always in the seizure-frequency section, and 
splitting encouraged the model to select wrong answers more 
often. No held-out artifact exists.

\emph{Sampling and orchestration on an older stack.} A 
predeclared July study on a 50-letter development panel with 
gpt-4.1-mini compared greedy decoding with self-consistency 
(temperature 0.7, majority vote), a tool-using single call, 
and two multi-call orchestrations. Majority voting scored 
15/50 against greedy 19/50. The multi-call variants scored 
29--32/50 but with 4--8 losses against greedy and failed the 
predeclared gate (wins $\geq$ 5 and losses $\leq$ 1). The 
study used an older model, 50 rows and a gate designed for a 
different sample size. The permitted reading is narrow: on 
this task the greedy answer is already the modal answer, so 
sampling adds noise; and dynamic orchestration was not tested 
at scale. It does not support the sentence ``multi-call 
pipelines do not help''.

\subsection{Hybrid Pipeline Variants}

The \texttt{Hybrid} row in the paper is one extraction call 
followed by a deterministic label rewrite and the decision 
rules. Two variants of that stack were measured on 
\texttt{test450}. Skipping the label rewrite so that the 
decision rules act directly on the model's own label forms 
scores 0.85 (382), five letters below the cited row. Adding 
rule-extracted candidates to the model's record before the 
rules decide scores 0.82 (368), below the model's record 
alone, so the cited row uses the model's record without 
rule-extracted additions.

The decision rules are nine named families applied in a 
fixed order. On \texttt{dev750} they change the answer on 67 
of 750 letters: 45 rescues, 4 harms, net +41 (608 to 649 
before the final family was added). The largest families are 
diary totals (+25), dated sequences (+10) and post-treatment 
bursts (+7). The last family added, a last-event rule that 
rewrites a short numeric seizure-free span after a day-dated 
last event, was accepted on development evidence and 
replayed once on the held-out set as part of the final 
configuration (656/750 development, 387/450 held-out). Each 
family, its authority and its portability scope are listed 
in the repository rule catalogue.

\section{Development vs. Test Results}

Figure~\ref{devtest} shows the two decision executors on 
both splits. \texttt{Hybrid} scores 0.87 (656/750) on 
development and 0.86 (387/450) on the held-out set, a 
generalisation gap of $-$0.01. \texttt{LLM-only} scores 0.85 
on both (640/750 and 383/450). Neither executor was tuned on 
held-out letters; prompts and rules were fixed on the 
development split and replayed once. The small gaps are 
consistent with the extraction call doing the pattern 
matching across the whole letter and the decision stage 
acting only on the refined record. The development-only 
rules-extraction baseline mentioned in the Development 
section showed the larger development-to-test drop typical of 
rules tuned on one corpus; it is not part of this comparison 
and is recorded in the repository.

In the early phases of development, comprehensive error 
analysis showed that the LLM
was consistently choosing valid answers based 
on the instructions. For example, the LLM is told
to choose the seizure frequency with the highest
current or recent seizure pattern, if multiple seizure 
events are available. When it sees a pattern such
as \emph{2 in Jan, 0 in Feb, 0 in March, 5 in April}
the model chooses 5 because it is the largest 
and most recent pattern. However the gold-standard
label is 7 in 4 months. When the prompt instruction
was changed to add up all seizure events in a period,
then it combined tonic-clonic seizures and generalised
seizures over overlapping periods, while the gold-standard
choose the highest count among the two seizure types.
Separating these complex, somewhat contradictory 
instructions into a separate decision stage, whether a 
second LLM call or rules, produced the +0.06 to +0.07 
improvement reported in the paper.

\section{Evidence Quality Beyond Exact Spans}

The paper measures evidence quality only as exact-substring 
adherence (Table~\ref{tab:adherence-supp}). An exact span is 
not proof that the span semantically supports the label. The 
gold standard's single reference quote is a loose comparator 
for that question: it may be exact, paraphrased, abbreviated, 
ellipsized or missing, so it is not ground truth for 
span correctness.

A development-first directional study is specified and 
drafted but not yet run. It measures two properties of the 
existing annotation reference on \texttt{dev750}: whether it 
can be located as an exact source substring, and whether it 
conveys enough meaning to support the target label. It 
measures two matching properties of the pipeline record: 
whether the collected spans occur in the letter, and whether 
all raw grouped spans, considered together, support the 
target label under the decision policy. The adjudicator for 
the pipeline arm receives only the target label, the raw 
grouped spans and the policy; it does not see the letter, the 
normalised labels, the predicted answer or the annotation 
reference. It returns \emph{supported}, \emph{unsupported} 
or \emph{insufficient}, a short reason, and controlled policy 
tags. A development pilot and an explicit acceptance decision 
must precede any aggregate-only replay on the held-out set. 
The study is a directional measurement and protocol check; it 
is not clinical validation.

\section{The Extraction Prompt}

The cited extraction request is the codebook prompt 
(implementation name \texttt{gan\_llm\_extract}). The object 
at the end of this document is the frozen template sent to the 
model: task, instructions, allowed label forms, event schema 
and selection schema. The four ingredients ablated in the 
paper map onto it as follows: the \emph{instructions} are the 
task and instruction blocks; the \emph{examples} are the 
worked examples inside the instructions; the \emph{allowed 
labels} are the closed \texttt{label\_forms} block; and the 
\emph{evidence obligation} is the evidence fields of the event 
schema together with the exact-quote instruction. The letter 
is attached at run time as \texttt{note\_text} and is omitted 
here. Research metadata such as \texttt{prompt\_version} is 
not part of the model-facing payload.

This template is not the source-near variant 
(\texttt{gan\_llm\_extract\_raw}), which has no closed 
\texttt{label\_forms} block, and it is not the second-call 
decision prompt used in \texttt{LLM-only}. Event categories 
are listed in Table~\ref{tab:event-categories} and label 
forms in Table~\ref{tab:label-forms}.

\section{Experimental Environment}

Experiments using local language models took place on a 
Dell XPS 16 9640 running Windows 11, with an Intel Core 
Ultra 9 185H, 32 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 
4070 Laptop GPU (8 GB VRAM). Ollama v0.32.15 was 
used to serve the two local models (Qwen 3.8 27B, Gemma 4 
26B). 

Experiments using hosted APIs were run from a Mac 
mini M1 with 16 GB unified memory on macOS 26.5.2. API
calls were made directly to the provider (OpenAI, DeepSeek) 
or through model routers (OpenRouter, AI Gateway). Four 
hosted models were used (Gemini 3.7 Flash, GPT-5.6 Luna, 
Grok 4.6, DeepSeek V4 Flash). Hosted accelerators are not 
disclosed by the providers.

All models used a temperature of 0 except Luna, which only 
accepts a temperature of 1. An ablation was run on Gemini 
using a temperature of 1. All models used a thinking or effort 
level of low. Ablations were run on Gemini using thinking 
levels of medium and high. 

Python 3.14.4 was used to write the program. DSPy 3.2.1, 
Pydantic 2.13.4 and LiteLLM 1.87.1 were the core packages. 
DSPy co-ordinated the model calls and required structured 
JSON output. Pydantic validated that the structured output 
conformed to the schema. LiteLLM delivered API calls to Ollama 
and the model routers. Every reported cell is replayable from 
saved model output in the repository's 
\texttt{paper\_experiments/} directory without a new model 
call.
