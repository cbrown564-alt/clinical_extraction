# Extended literature review — working draft

## Complexity of clinical narratives

Clinical letters use narrative language because clinicians need to describe the patient’s situation, their judgement, and uncertainty. This is useful for clinical communication, but it is harder to reuse than a fixed data field [Rosenbloom et al., 2011]. Clinical writing often includes hedge terms for probability, frequency, and quantity, rather than a precise assertion [Hanauer et al., 2012]. Information extraction must turn this flexible language into structured data without treating uncertainty as certainty.

This challenge is especially visible in epilepsy clinic letters. A single letter can contain diagnoses, prescriptions, patient history, seizure descriptions, and several references to seizure frequency. Fonferko-Shadrach et al. (2024) found markedly lower human inter-annotator agreement for seizure frequency (per-item F1 = 0.47) than for prescriptions (0.87) or diagnoses (0.83). Their analysis links this difficulty to the wide variety of frequency expressions and to references to multiple seizure or event types; the final gold standard was reached through annotator consensus. Seizure-frequency extraction consequently requires more than locating relevant words: it requires an explicit policy for deciding which evidence represents the target fact and for applying that policy consistently.

## Seizure frequency as a selection task

Epilepsy letters can support different information extraction tasks. ExECT and the related annotation work treat a letter as a clinical inventory: the system identifies several entity mentions and records their attributes. For example, one letter may contain several antiseizure medications, with a name, dose, unit, and administration frequency for each medication [Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2019; Fonferko-Shadrach et al., 2024]. This representation is appropriate when the aim is to preserve a broad account of the information in the letter.

The seizure-frequency task used in this study has a different output. The dataset created by Gan et al. (2026) assigns each letter one label for the patient’s current seizure-frequency status. The task is therefore not to retain every seizure-frequency mention. Instead, the system must identify the relevant candidate descriptions, interpret their meaning and time frame, and select the single normalised label that best represents the current pattern. This one-label formulation is also used in previous seizure-frequency studies [Xie et al., 2022; Holgate et al., 2024; Holgate et al., 2025]. It motivates separating the work into finding candidate evidence, encoding it, and selecting the final answer.

## Rules, fine-tuned models, and LLMs

Published seizure-frequency systems use several different approaches. Rules-based systems identify terms and apply domain-specific logic. ExECT demonstrates this approach for broad clinical inventory extraction, while Decker et al. (2022) use regular-expression rules for seizure-related variables. Fine-tuned transformer models learn patterns from labelled examples instead; Xie et al. (2022) and Fernandes et al. (2024) provide examples for seizure-frequency extraction. The comparative-results table in the supporting materials summarises the F1 scores reported by these studies. Those scores should be read in the context of each study’s dataset, target labels, and evaluation method, rather than as a direct ranking of methods.

More recent work uses large language models either through prompting or task-specific fine-tuning. Holgate et al. (2024) show that a frozen model can extract seizure information when given instructions and examples. Later fine-tuned LLM studies, including Holgate et al. (2025) and Gan et al. (2026), use labelled data to adapt a model to a defined output format. The published results show that rules, fine-tuned models, and prompted LLMs can all be useful, but they make different trade-offs. Rules expose their logic, fine-tuned models need labelled data and training, and prompted LLMs depend on prompt design and output controls. This leaves an open design question: whether rules and LLMs should be treated as alternatives, or assigned to different stages of the same extraction pipeline.

## Modular and sequential extraction pipelines

Multi-stage clinical extraction pipelines separate a task into distinct operations rather than asking a model for one final answer in a single step. CLINES is the closest example: it uses stages for chunking, extraction, attribute assignment, normalisation, date resolution, and aggregation [Yang et al., 2025]. This is best understood as a sequential pipeline with repeated model calls, rather than a team of agents debating an answer. Epilepsy studies have also used decomposed prompting, including numbered reasoning steps and summarise-then-extract designs [Holgate et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2025]. These studies motivate staged processing, but they do not show that adding steps is always beneficial.

Most prior work compares rules, fine-tuned models, and prompted LLMs as competing end-to-end approaches. The pipeline in this study instead makes three operations configurable: finding candidate evidence, encoding it into a structured form, and selecting the final answer. Each operation can use rules, an LLM call, or a hybrid of both. The contribution is therefore not a claim that a longer sequence is better. It is a scored comparison of configurations that tests where decomposition and each component are useful for a one-label seizure-frequency task.
